What is real here, and what is not

Every sprite on this screen is now computed by the port. Wave 44 put the port's own hardware display list — the one it has been building at $800000 every frame since wave 11 and nothing ever read — on the glass. The ship, its pods, its shadows and the enemies are simulated: position, velocity, tilt, clamps and speed modes from the board's own arithmetic, one logic frame per 16.896 ms, 0 divergent frames against the board over 2,200 logic frames on 34 compared columns. What is still the recording is the HUD text and the palette, and the page says so below.

The background SCROLLS LIVE now — wave 13. The whole scroll subsystem is ported: object type 1 ($26127A), its seven-opcode program interpreter ($262062), the repeat/unfreeze partner ($261F76), the entry-clock fast-forward ($26200E), both cameras with their 1/64-pixel fractions ($240B94/$240C22), the register upload and the tilemap ring writer ($240D76). The camera position, the scroll registers and every map column now come from the port's own arithmetic reading the cartridge's own data, not from the recording — 0 divergent frames against the board over 10,431 logic frames, the whole of stage 1 from the first record to the boss lock, on twelve compared columns, with nine deliberate mistakes watched go red first. Stage 1 is 7,317 logic frames and 8,486 pixels; the recording was 161 frames and 160 pixels of it.

And wave 14 gave it the PICTURE. Wave 13 scrolled the whole stage but the bundle only held the 415 background tiles harvested from the recording — 160 pixels of an 8,486 pixel stage — so past the recording the screen went black. All 1,820 tiles the 224 scrolling map columns reference are now exported straight from the cartridge's own layout data ($225B78), together with the 205 of the separate 23-column map at $227AF8 and the stage's 2,048-byte palette block ($227E58, 32 banks of 32). They arrive in eight shards: the page fetches two before the first frame — less than it used to fetch in total — and pulls the other six in while you play, in the order the scroll program itself says it will need them. If one is still in flight the status line says WAITING ON SHARD n and those columns are transparent rather than wrong; if one fails to arrive the page stops and names the file. A blank column that says so is honest; a black screen that says nothing is what got reported.

Two things are still not the cartridge's. The palette that draws is still the recording's: the real block is shipped and checked (1,020 of its 1,024 entries are byte-identical to what the board had), but four entries are animated by a routine nobody has ported, so the recording's copy is the one with the animation in it. And the second map's painter ($26C20C, object type $1C) is unported — its tiles are in the bundle, nothing draws them yet.

The recorded enemies have been taken off the screen — and the port's own are on it. Until wave 37 every enemy, bullet, explosion and item you saw was a 161-frame recording of the real board, on a loop — and since wave 13 the background's motion has been computed by the port instead of replayed alongside it. A 161-frame sprite loop played against a 7,317-frame computed stage agrees at the start and drifts afterwards, which is what it looked like: enemies shooting at nothing, in the wrong place. So they went: 7,671 of the recording's display-list records dropped to 886, 8.99% of the picture's pixels. Nothing you can see now is a recorded sprite.

Wave 44 filled the hole it left. The port has built the real hardware display list every frame since wave 11 — main-loop call #4, $23D2AE, translated whole and verified byte-for-byte against the board on 1,901 consecutive frames — and it has spawned, moved, aimed and shot its enemies for eight waves. Nothing read that list. Now the page does: it copies $800000..$8009FF out of the port's own RAM one frame late, exactly as the sprite DMA does, translates each record's cartridge stream address into the shipped sheet's, and draws it. Measured from this page's own seed: 16,457 display-list records over 300 logic frames, 20–69 per frame, and bucket 0 — the enemies — runs 14 to 62 records a frame.

A record whose picture is not in the bundle is not drawn, and its cartridge address goes on the status line. No guess, no nearest match, no drawing it anyway. The shipped sheet was harvested from a 161-frame recording, so it covers this seed's play completely for the first 5.32 seconds — 0 missing records — and then starts thinning out, biggest pictures first: the very first record with no art is $233F34, a 5×80 background element, at logic frame 2315. After that the status line says NO ART n: and lists the addresses. That degradation is a property of this seed, which is the recording's own window; it is not a general guarantee, and shipping the missing art is the next wave. The HUD, the score and the text are still the recording — they are the text layer, not sprites, and none of this touched them.

The ship and everything attached to it are now PRODUCED, not relocated. Wave 12 ported the ship's own draw block $24A482 and the whole option object $24C096 as far as the laser, so the eight records are computed every frame: the ship, its two option pods, its invulnerability aura, its exhaust glow and the three ground shadows. Every byte of all eight — position, image, size, flip and colour — is compared against the board's own staged bucket bytes AND against the display-list entries they become, 0 divergent frames over 2,200 logic frames (pgm.py shipgate), and ten deliberate mistakes were watched go red on that gate first. The ship banks. It has computed its own tilt since wave 4 and could never draw it, because the bundle's sprite streams are re-based and the map was not shipped; the 17 rebased image pairs are in the manifest now, and 16 of them are not in the recording at all — the recorded ship never banked, so they were read out of the sprite ROMs by address.

What the recording still supplies for these eight is which display-list slot they occupy, and nothing else. That is the last thread, and it is not a property of the ship: it is a property of the other twenty-six sprite buckets, which have no simulated source yet. Until they do, the page cannot build the whole list, so the port's records are written into the recorded one.

Until wave 9 only three of the eight were moved, and a player found the other five by looking. The matcher was asking the wrong question: "is this record at a constant offset from the ship in at least 90 % of frames?" The exhaust and the shadows are drawn on alternate frames — which is how this hardware faked transparency — so they can never score above half, and the shadows are not at a constant offset at all: they sit on a ground plane, halfway between the object and a fixed point, which is what $249E7E computes. The test now asks "given the record is there at all, is it where the ship says?" — eight records score 100 %, the best thing it rejects scores 58.6 %, and it prints the rejects so the next person can see what it chose not to take.

The exhaust and the shadow flicker, and that is the board. The exhaust is drawn on odd frames, the shadow on even frames, and no frame carries both. On the arcade CRT that reads as translucency; on a 60 Hz LCD it may read as strobing instead. This page reproduces it exactly rather than drawing them solidly at half opacity, because that would look better and be something the board never did. If it bothers you, that is a decision worth making deliberately — it is written up, not overlooked.

Your ship banks now, and here is why it did not before. The port has computed the tilt and the tilt-indexed image words since wave 4. What was missing was not the arithmetic: it was the map. The exporter re-bases every sprite stream into a packed 16-bit space to keep the bundle small, so the port's ROM-space image addresses pointed at nothing the page had — and sixteen of the seventeen bank images were not in the bundle at all, because the recorded ship's X never moved (one single value on all 161 frames) and the recording therefore contains only the level-flight image. Wave 12 harvests the other sixteen out of the sprite ROMs by address and ships the 17 rebased pairs in manifest.ship. A limitation that looked like a missing feature was a missing table.

No weapon is drawn and there is no sound at all. What the fire buttons actually do, which is three different things:

  • TAP shot — really runs the ported cadence machine ($249B2C..$249BE2), the ported spawn ($249BFC), the 36-slot shot driver ($253A70) and the sprite enqueue ($23F3AE), all verified 0-divergent against the board over 125 logic frames on 52 columns. You will not see it. None of the nine shot sprite streams is in the shipped sheet, so a shot the port really computed still has no picture — and after wave 44 the status line says so by address rather than leaving you to guess. The enemies it would hit are real objects now, and they are the ones on the screen.
  • HOLD shot — on the board this is the laser, and it also ramps the ship's speed down from index 22 to 12. Neither is ported: the ramp lives inside the option object at $24C8BE. Holding for four frames stops the loop with that address. Before wave 9 it did nothing at all and said nothing, which was worse.
  • BOMB — reaches a named throw at $249814 and stops the loop.
  • AUTO — Button 3 is not a third weapon; the board synthesises a shot edge on alternate frames. It does not touch the speed ramp, so it is the one way to keep firing without stopping the port.

A red box naming a $ address is not a crash. It is the port saying which ROM routine it reached that is not ported yet, and stopping rather than inventing the next frame. The address is the useful part.

TATE and WIDE

The cabinet is TATE: MAME's driver declares the screen rotate="270" on a 448x224 buffer, so the correct picture is 224 wide by 448 tall, and that is the default everywhere. WIDE shows the raw board buffer unrotated — the game lying on its side. It is a preference, not a correction, and it is remembered. Rotating does not change a single logic frame; both modes scale by a whole number of device pixels, because the rotation is done in the pixel buffer and never by a CSS transform.

Controls

←↑↓→ or WASD move   Z/Y/Space shot   X bomb   C auto   Enter start   L sprite source: the PORT'S own display list (default) or the recording's

That last one is a diagnostic, not a feature, and it is on the page because it is the cheapest correctness check this port has that does not need an emulator: the ship must land in exactly the same place in both. In port every sprite you can see is computed by the port; in capture the sprite layer is the recording's, with wave 37's strip applied. The simulation is identical either way — the key changes the picture, never a logic frame.

How much of this is measured, and by what

Every number on this page came out of a command recorded in docs/worklog/ddpdoj/, run against the real board image under MAME 0.288.

  • The ship: pgm.py flyaround — 0 divergent frames, 34 compared columns, 2,200 logic frames (wave 4).
  • The shot: pgm.py shotgate — 0 divergent frames, 52 columns, 125 logic frames, and every one of the 574 sprite requests the port emitted appears verbatim in the board's own queue (wave 8).
  • The renderer: pgm.py pixslice — 13,647,872/13,647,872 pixels over 136 frame pairs, with nine deliberate mutations all going red (wave 6). Two of those nine stayed green on the previous corpus, which is how the project found out that palette timing and sprite priority had never been tested at all.
  • The whole demo path: pgm.py demogate — 15,955,968/15,955,968 pixels over 159 frames, port-driven ship included.
  • This page's asset bundle: node games/ddpdoj/tools/bundlegate.mjs — the same 15,955,968/15,955,968, rendered from the 363 KiB published bundle rather than from 58 MiB of cartridge, with four deliberate breaks that all go red.

What is not measured, said plainly: the enemies you can see are a recording; sound is not implemented at all; and no part of this page has ever been run in a browser by the people who wrote it — every check above is headless. If something here looks wrong, it probably is, and saying so is useful.
